# ISTOE — Incentive Structures Theory of Everything

**A universal root cause analysis engine.**

ISTOE is a way of finding the real cause of a problem. Instead of asking
"what's wrong with the people?", it asks a different question: **"what
behavior is this rule-set mathematically rewarding?"**

That one question, asked at every scale — one person, one team, one company,
one country — is the whole framework. "Everything" here means *at every
scale*, not every phenomenon explained. ISTOE is a diagnostic framework, a
method for root-cause analysis. Its test is usefulness, not completeness.

This repo is the public home for the ISTOE documentation.

---

## Executive summary — layperson

Most problems we blame on people are actually caused by the rules those
people are operating under. A call center that scores reps on how fast they
end calls will get reps who rush people off the phone — not because the reps
are lazy, but because the scoring system pays them, in effect, to do exactly
that. Your phone eats your evening for the same reason: every app on it is
built to keep you scrolling, because attention is what it gets paid for.

ISTOE is the habit of looking past the person and at the setup. Find the
rules. Find what the rules reward. Follow that reward to the end of the
line. Nine times out of ten, the behavior that looks like a character flaw
turns out to be the rational response to a badly designed system.

The point isn't to excuse anyone. The point is that if you want different
behavior, you don't fix the people — you fix the rules, so the behavior you
want becomes the easy, default thing to do. And you judge the result by the
worst-off person in the system, not the average: a system scores by its
minimum, not its mean. Raise the floor.

## Executive summary — systems thinker

ISTOE generalizes root-cause analysis, dependency mapping, and telemetry
troubleshooting into a scale-invariant diagnostic framework — a semantic
index of incentives from micro to macro. Its diagnostic rule: map every
stakeholder without truncation, trace each incentive to its terminal goal,
and ask what behavior the rule-set mathematically rewards, rather than
pathologizing the agents operating under it.

Its four pillars: (1) energy-return ratio — action is finite biological
currency, and collapsed ROI yields rational disengagement, not pathology;
(2) upstream architecture vs. downstream blame — discourse pathologizes
individuals to protect systems; (3) asymmetric penalties and mismatched
interfaces — neurotypical-default systems punish jagged profiles for
navigation failure, not capability; (4) macro-economic game theory —
corporate behavior and wealth consolidation as rational responses to rule
changes.

It is a method, not a model. It carries five stated concessions: motivation
is plural and dissociable; incentives change the preferences they act on;
perfect alignment is impossible by theorem; no neutral mapper exists; and
"raise the floor" is a stated moral choice, not a derived one. Its
objective is to align incentive structures so desired behavior becomes the
system's default equilibrium, and to judge a system by its floor — quality
of life, lifespan, and healthspan for the worst-off — rather than its mean.

## For the layperson

### The idea in plain language

Everything people do is a response to incentives — the rewards and penalties
built into the situation they're in. Change the incentives and the behavior
changes, whether or not anyone's character changes. That's true for a kid
doing chores for allowance, a warehouse crew gaming a productivity metric,
and a corporation buying back its own stock. Same question, different scale.

### Two everyday examples

**The call center.** Reps are scored on average call time, so they cut
people off and transfer them around. Management says the reps have a bad
attitude. The reps are doing exactly what the scoring system rewards: short
calls. The attitude is downstream. The metric is upstream.

**Your phone.** You meant to check one message and lost forty minutes. That
isn't a willpower failure. The apps are engineered to hold your attention,
because attention is the product they sell. The loop is: notification,
scroll, small reward, repeat. You are running a system someone else designed
to win against your evening.

### How to use it

1. **Set the blame aside.** Not forever — just for the analysis. Blame is
   noise that stops you from seeing the mechanism.
2. **Name the symptom.** What is actually happening, in plain words?
3. **List everyone involved.** Not just the people being blamed. Everyone
   who touches the system, including the people who wrote the rules.
4. **Ask what each person is rewarded for.** Follow the reward to the end
   of the line — what does this person actually get out of doing this?
5. **Name the pattern.** Is the system rewarding the thing it says it
   wants, or the opposite? Most broken systems reward the opposite.
6. **Change the rules, not the people.** Redesign so the behavior you want
   is the default — the thing that happens when nobody tries hard.
7. **Check the floor.** Did the worst-off person in the system get a better
   deal? If not, the fix didn't work yet.

### What it changes

It changes where you look. Instead of arguing about who is good or bad,
lazy or motivated, you look at the setup — and setups can be edited. That's
the payoff: reality stops being a black box you can only complain about and
becomes a schematic you can actually change.

## For the systems thinker

### The four pillars

1. **Energy-return ratio.** Time, attention, and bandwidth are finite
   biological currency. When the return on effort collapses, disengagement
   is the rational response — lying flat, burnout, quiet quitting — not a
   defect in the agent.
2. **Upstream architecture vs. downstream blame.** Discourse defaults to
   pathologizing individuals. That default protects the system that produced
   the behavior.
3. **Asymmetric penalties and mismatched interfaces.** Systems built for a
   neurotypical default punish jagged profiles for navigation failure, not
   for lack of capability. The interface is the penalty.
4. **Macro-economic game theory.** Corporate behavior is a rational response
   to rule changes. Example: SEC Rule 10b-18 (adopted November 17, 1982)
   legalized stock buybacks, and buybacks followed the rule change, not a
   change in corporate character.

### The diagnostic template

Run this first pass on any system, before the deeper mapping:

- **Inputs** — what drives the behavior? (e.g., high-potency core values
  colliding)
- **Architecture** — what are the pathways and delivery mechanisms? (e.g.,
  an outdated approval process as the old pipeline)
- **Constraints** — what are the hardware limits? (e.g., time, attention,
  and energy budgets)
- **Output** — what is the final state? (e.g., burnout, anxiety, depression)

Inputs map to stakeholder incentives and terminal goals. Architecture maps to
rule-sets, interfaces, and loops. Constraints map to the energy-return ratio
and finite currency. Output maps to downstream symptoms.

### Terminal currency

All behavior is filtered through the brain's reward center, in nested game
loops across timescales — minute-to-minute, hour-to-hour, day-to-day. The
terminal vocabulary: emotional valence; self-determination (autonomy,
competence, relatedness); and meaning as pleasure, where "meaning" is the
label for the loops that pay off over the longest horizons. Two corrections
to the naive version: wanting is not liking (Berridge & Robinson), and
hedonic and eudaimonic motivation are dissociable — a unitary "pleasure"
fails as stated.

### The mental-map constellation

Three patterns recur across domains and anchor the v1.0 index:

- **Reward hacking** — universal and evolutionary, not just an AI problem.
  Maximum reward for minimum effort is a subsistence legacy; engineered
  stimuli in abundance hijack the survival loop. Self-hacking vs.
  other-hacking; proxy cascades.
- **Maximum output, minimum input** — human nature as an optimization
  pressure, not a moral fact.
- **Continuity bias** — a strong bias toward continuity of systems rather
  than adaptation or change; the mechanism behind the transition problem,
  norm inertia, and policy-feedback lock-in.

### Known failure modes

The framework is indexed against its own failure modes, including:
reward hacking and proxy cascades; Goodhart/Campbell effects; crowding-out
(incentives changing the preferences they act on — Gneezy & Rustichini's
daycare fine); preference falsification; within-agent loop conflicts;
stakeholder-map truncation (stopping at instrumental goals instead of
terminal ones); the transition problem; and the impossibility results —
Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, Myerson–Satterthwaite — which bound what any
alignment can achieve.

### The five concessions

Stated up front, as the scope statement's contents:

1. Motivation is plural and dissociable; a unitary pleasure theory fails.
2. Incentives change the preferences, norms, and identity they act on, and
   the change is sometimes irreversible.
3. Perfect mapping and alignment are impossible by theorem.
4. No neutral mapper exists; every mapping is positioned.
5. "Raise the floor" and "genuine satisfaction" are contested moral choices,
   and their measurement is Goodhart-vulnerable.

Absorbing these demotes a Theory of Everything to a strong, falsifiable
programme — which is a promotion, not a defeat.

### The method

0. **Moral impotence (step zero).** Morality is social norms assigned higher
   priority, and it is downstream of incentive structures. Strip the moral
   and emotional charge as a debiasing exercise so the mechanism can be
   examined logically.
1. Name the symptom (Output).
2. Map every stakeholder without truncation.
3. Trace each incentive to its terminal goal.
4. Identify the pattern (reward hacking, energy-return collapse,
   crowding-out, continuity bias, mismatched interface, truncation).
5. Check the known failure modes.
6. Treat the system itself as a design variable; redesign so cooperation is
   the default equilibrium.
7. Judge by the floor test: quality of life, lifespan, and healthspan for
   the worst-off.

### The objective

Align **all** stakeholders' incentive structures so desired behavior becomes
the system's default. Solutions are not parties or platforms; alignment is
nonpartisan by construction. A system's score is its minimum, not its mean —
raise the floor, the baseline of human existence.
